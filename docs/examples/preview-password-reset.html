Password reset

6 steps · 4 rounds · about 165,000 tokens of work

A rough total of what each step said it would cost, counting steps that run together once. It is an estimate nobody has measured, not a promise.

What we're going to do

Right now a customer who forgets their password has to email support and wait, and somebody on our side resets it by hand. We are building the self-service version: the customer asks for a reset, gets a link by email, and chooses a new password themselves. Support stops being in the middle of it. The work is six steps and we expect it to take about a week and a half.

Why we're doing it

Password resets are the single most common thing support is asked to do, and every one of them costs a person twenty minutes and the customer half a day of waiting. It is also the riskiest thing we do by hand, because the person doing it has to decide, on the spot, whether the customer is who they say they are.

What will be different afterwards

Customers reset their own password without talking to anybody, at any hour. Support keeps the ability to see that a reset happened, and when, but no longer performs them. Nobody has to judge a stranger's identity over email any more.

How the work is split up

The work is split into 6 steps. They run in 4 rounds: everything in a round happens at the same time, and a round starts only when the one before it has finished.

Round 1

Steps 1 and 2 run at the same time.

  • Step 1 — The one-time link that expires
  • Step 2 — The email the customer reads

Round 2

Step 3 runs on its own. It begins once round 1 has finished.

  • Step 3 — Asking for a reset, and finishing one

Round 3

Steps 4 and 5 run at the same time. It begins once round 2 has finished.

  • Step 4 — The page the customer sees
  • Step 5 — Stopping it being used to send junk

Round 4

Step 6 runs on its own. It begins once round 3 has finished.

  • Step 6 — Telling support before customers do

Each step, in detail

Step 1 — The one-time link that expires

Happens at the same time as step 2. Nothing has to finish before it starts.

What it does

Creates the single-use link a reset email carries, and makes it stop working after thirty minutes or after it has been used once.

Why it matters

This link is the only thing standing between a stranger and somebody's account. If it can be reused or guessed, everything else is theatre.

What changes

Two new files, both new. Nothing existing is touched.

What might go wrong

Low in the sense that nothing today depends on it, and high in the sense that it has to be right — it is the security boundary for the feature.

How we'll know it worked

Tests that deliberately use a link twice, and use one that has aged past thirty minutes, and confirm both are refused.

acceptance criteria

  2. A token older than thirty minutes is refused, and the message says it expired.

  3. Tokens are unguessable: 32 random bytes, never a counter.

  4. Nothing is logged that would let a reader reconstruct a token.

Step 2 — The email the customer reads

Happens at the same time as step 1. Nothing has to finish before it starts.

What it does

Writes the email itself: who asked for the reset, from what device, when, and what to do if it was not them.

Why it matters

This is the only part of the feature most customers will consciously read. If it looks like a phishing email, people will not trust it, and the ones who should be worried will not notice.

What changes

Two new files. No existing mail is changed.

What might go wrong

Low. It sends nothing until step 3 connects it.

How we'll know it worked

Tests confirm the plain-text and HTML versions say the same thing, and that the reset link is the only clickable item in the message.

Step 3 — Asking for a reset, and finishing one

Nothing else runs at the same time as this step. Waits for steps 1 and 2 to finish first.

What it does

The two operations behind the scenes: take a request for a reset, and accept a new password when the customer comes back with their link.

Why it matters

This is where the privacy mistake would happen. Asking to reset an address that has no account must look exactly like asking to reset one that does, or anybody can use the form to find out who our customers are.

What changes

Two new files. It waits for steps 1 and 2 because it uses both.

What might go wrong

Medium. Finishing a reset signs the customer out everywhere else, which is correct but will surprise anyone with a second device open.

How we'll know it worked

A test asks to reset an address that does not exist and confirms the answer is indistinguishable from a real one.

Happens at the same time as step 5. Waits for step 3 to finish first.

Step 5 — Stopping it being used to send junk

Happens at the same time as step 4. Waits for step 3 to finish first.

What it does

Caps how many reset emails one address, or one sender, can trigger in an hour.

Why it matters

Without it, our reset form is a free way to send somebody else a hundred emails they did not ask for. It happens to every service that forgets this.

What changes

Two new files. It runs at the same time as step 4 because they touch nothing in common.

What might go wrong

Low, with one judgement call: a customer who genuinely tries six times in an hour is refused the sixth. We think that is the right trade.

How we'll know it worked

A test makes six requests and confirms the sixth is throttled, and that a throttled answer looks the same as a successful one.

What could go wrong

The dangerous mistake in a feature like this is building something that quietly tells a stranger whether an email address has an account with us. We have written that into the steps as a thing to check rather than trusting we will remember. The second risk is the reset email being used to send unwanted mail to somebody else, which is what step 5 is for.

What could go wrong in each step

  • Step 1 — The one-time link that expires

    Low in the sense that nothing today depends on it, and high in the sense that it has to be right — it is the security boundary for the feature.

  • Step 2 — The email the customer reads

    Low. It sends nothing until step 3 connects it.

  • Step 3 — Asking for a reset, and finishing one

    Medium. Finishing a reset signs the customer out everywhere else, which is correct but will surprise anyone with a second device open.

  • Step 4 — The page the customer sees

    Low. It is the surface of work already done and tested underneath.

  • Step 5 — Stopping it being used to send junk

    Low, with one judgement call: a customer who genuinely tries six times in an hour is refused the sixth. We think that is the right trade.

  • Step 6 — Telling support before customers do

    Low.

What we are not doing

We are not changing how people sign in normally, not adding two-factor authentication, and not touching passwords that already exist. Somebody who remembers their password sees nothing different.

What the words mean

Step
One piece of the work, with its own list of files and its own checks. Steps are numbered in the order they can start.
Round
A group of steps that all happen at the same time. The next round begins only when every step in this one has finished.
At the same time
Two steps that run together, on different files, so the whole job finishes sooner.
Token
Roughly three quarters of a word of reading or writing. The totals on this page are a rough guess at how much reading and writing the work will take. It is not a price and not a promise.
Checks
Automatic tests and human sign-offs that have to pass before a step counts as finished.
Technical detail
The switch at the top of this page. It reveals the file names, the checks and the exact wording each step was given. Nothing is hidden from you; it is folded away because most readers do not need it.
